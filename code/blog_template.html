Almost everything you do online crosses the sea floor on a cable about as thick as a garden hose. I mapped every one of them in TeleGeography's open record: when each was laid, who owns them now, and the narrow places where dozens squeeze through together.

Ask people how the internet crosses an ocean and quite a few will say satellites. In fact almost all of it goes through glass fibres in cables on the sea floor, laid by specialist ships from one beach to another. The best open record of these cables is TeleGeography's Submarine Cable Map, and its data are published for anyone to use. In its 2026 version there are {{n_active|int}} cables in service, with a combined length of {{km_active|int}} km, enough to go round the Earth {{times_round_earth|f1}} times. Another {{n_planned|int}} are planned or being laid.

A boom, a bust and a second boom

Each cable has a ready-for-service year, the year it started carrying traffic. The oldest still in use date from {{first_year|raw}}, and {{n_1990s_active|int}} cables from the 1990s are still working. Then comes the dot-com bubble: {{dotcom_n|int}} of today's cables came into service in just three years, 1999 to 2001, and {{km_peak_year|raw}} is still the year with the most kilometres laid ({{km_peak|int}} km). When the bubble burst, only {{bust_n|int}} cables came into service in the next three years.

Coral: planned cables by expected year. Retired cables are not in the data, so the early bars count survivors.}}

The second boom is now. {{peak_year|raw}} had the most new cables of any year ({{peak_n|int}}), and {{n_2027|int}} are planned for 2027. Cables are getting longer too: the median planned cable is {{median_km_by_era.planned|int}} km, against {{median_km_by_era.1990s|int}} km for the 1990s cables still in service. One caution: TeleGeography maps the cables in service and planned, not the ones switched off, so the early years show survivors, not everything that was laid.

From telephone clubs to cloud companies

Submarine cables used to be built by clubs of telephone companies, each taking a share: one cable in the record, {{consortia.max_owner_cable|raw}}, has {{consortia.max_owners|int}} owners. Then a new kind of owner arrived. I looked for Google, Meta, Amazon Web Services and Microsoft in each cable's list of owners, matching whole names only. They own a share of none of the length ready before 2010. Of the length ready from 2015 to 2019 they part-own {{hyper.share_km_1519|pct}}, from 2020 to 2026 {{hyper.share_km_2020s|pct}}, and of everything planned {{hyper.share_planned_km|pct}}.

{{IMG|b_hyperscalers.png|Horizontal bars of the share of new cable length part-owned by Google, Meta, Amazon or Microsoft: 0 per cent before 2010, 9 per cent in 2010 to 2014, 24 per cent in 2015 to 2019, 50 per cent in 2020 to 2026 and 55 per cent of planned length; beside it, Google part-owns 37 cables, Meta 21, Microsoft 8 and Amazon 6|The cloud companies' share of new cable length, and the number of cables each part-owns.}}

More and more they go it alone: {{hyper.sole|int}} of the {{hyper.n|int}} cables with one of the four as an owner have no other owner, and {{hyper.sole_planned|int}} of those are still planned. The longest cable in the whole record, the planned {{longest.0.name|raw}} at {{longest.0.km|int}} km, is Meta's alone. And these numbers are a floor, because the owners list does not show the capacity these companies buy on cables they do not own.

Where the network squeezes through

Hundreds of cables sound like plenty of spare routes, but geography funnels them. Anchors, fishing gear and earthquakes break cables every year, and where many share one strait or one stretch of coast, one accident can cut several at once. So I drew a band across each well-known strait and counted the cables whose routes cross it, and did the same for a ring of famous landing hubs.

{{IMG|b_chokepoints.png|Two bar charts. Straits: Strait of Sicily 18 cables in service plus 5 planned, Red Sea 13 plus 4, Bab el-Mandeb 12 plus 4, Luzon Strait and Strait of Malacca 10 each, Egypt crossing 9, Strait of Hormuz 5. Hubs: Singapore 38 plus 14 planned, Boso peninsula in Japan 20, Fujairah and Guam 15, Hong Kong and Cornwall 14|Cables in service (blue) and planned (coral) through each strait and reaching each hub.}}

The Red Sea is the great funnel between Europe and Asia. {{zone.bab.n_active|int}} cables in service pass through the Bab el-Mandeb, the strait at its southern end, with a combined length of {{zone.bab.km_active|int}} km, {{bab_share_km|pct}} of all the cable length in service. {{zone.bab.n_planned|int}} more are planned. To reach the Mediterranean, {{zone.egypt.n_active|int}} of them cross Egypt overland. In 2024 several Red Sea cables were damaged, reportedly by the dragging anchor of a ship that had been attacked and abandoned, and traffic between Europe, Asia and East Africa had to be rerouted.

{{IMG|b_redsea.png|Map from Sicily to the Gulf of Oman with cables through the Red Sea in colour: a thick bundle runs from the Strait of Sicily along the North African coast, crosses Egypt near Suez, runs the length of the Red Sea, squeezes through the Bab el-Mandeb and fans out into the Arabian Sea, with counting bands outlined in coral|The Red Sea funnel: in colour, the cables that pass the Bab el-Mandeb or cross Egypt.}}

The busiest strait in my list is the Strait of Sicily, the Mediterranean's waist, with {{zone.sicily.n_active|int}} cables. In East Asia the Luzon Strait between Taiwan and the Philippines carries {{zone.luzon.n_active|int}}, and in 2006 an earthquake off southern Taiwan broke several cables there at once. Then there are the hubs where cables crowd onto a few beaches. Singapore is in a class of its own, with {{zone.singapore.n_active|int}} cables reaching the sea within 35 km and {{zone.singapore.n_planned|int}} more planned. Guam ({{zone.guam.n_active|int}}) and Hawaii ({{zone.hawaii.n_active|int}}) are the stepping stones of the Pacific, and Cornwall ({{zone.cornwall.n_active|int}}) is still one of Europe's great landing coasts.

One cable from the world

Counting only the cables that link a country to another country, the United States has the most ({{top_countries.0.n|int}}), then the United Kingdom ({{top_countries.1.n|int}}) and Singapore ({{top_countries.2.n|int}}). At the other end, {{n_single|int}} countries and territories have just one. For places with land borders that matters less, but {{n_single_island|int}} of them are islands with no land border at all: Greenland, Nauru, Niue, Vanuatu, the Solomon Islands, St Helena and others. Tuvalu appears only on a planned cable.

{{IMG|b_connected.png|Left, bars of international cables per country, led by the United States with 59, the United Kingdom 51 and Singapore 34. Right, a list of 17 islands with a single international cable, from Anguilla and Christmas Island to Vanuatu and Wallis and Futuna, each with the name of its cable|The most connected countries, and the islands that hang on one international cable.}}

Tonga showed what that means. In January 2022 the eruption of the Hunga Tonga volcano cut the cable to Fiji that was then the country's only international link, and the islands were largely cut off for weeks. The record now lists Tonga on a second system too.

How I did this

Everything comes from TeleGeography's Submarine Cable Map data (its routes, landing points and a record for each cable), with land and borders from Natural Earth. Length is TeleGeography's stated length, or the drawn route where none is given. Owners were matched by exact name. For the choke points I counted cables whose drawn routes cross a band across each strait or enter an area round each hub, and as a check counted the cables crossing every half-degree cell of a world grid. The caveats matter: TeleGeography draws routes schematically, retired cables are not included, and there are no capacities, so a 1990s cable counts the same as a new one that carries thousands of times more. This is a map of where cables run, not of how much they carry.
